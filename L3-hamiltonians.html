In the first two chapters, we estimated the expectation values of single Pauli strings on noisy devices. In this chapter, we turn to the observables and the dynamics of physical systems. Both are described by a Hamiltonian, the matrix of the energy of a system, which on a quantum computer becomes a sum of many Pauli strings. These sums stay manageable because physical Hamiltonians are local: each term acts on a few nearby qubits.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to write a Hamiltonian as a sum of Pauli strings; estimate its energy with a given number of shots and few measurement settings; build circuits for time evolution from native gates and count their cost; and weigh the error of such a circuit against the noise of its gates.

3.1 Energy and time evolution

The Hamiltonian \(H\) of a system is the observable of its energy. On \(n\) qubits, it is a Hermitian \(2^n\times 2^n\) matrix. Its eigenvalues \(E_k\) are the values that a measurement of the energy can give, and its eigenvectors \(\ket{E_k}\), the states with a definite energy, solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation \[ H\ket{E_k} = E_k\ket{E_k} . \] The lowest eigenvalue is the ground-state energy, and its eigenvector the ground state. They matter because a system that is cold enough settles in its ground state, or close to it, so the ground state decides how a material behaves at low temperature, for example whether it is magnetic. Differences between energies decide much of chemistry: whether a molecule is stable, how much energy a reaction needs, which light a molecule absorbs. Finding ground states is the subject of the next chapter.

The Hamiltonian also decides how a state changes in time, through the time-dependent Schrödinger equation and its solution, \[ i\,\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)} = H\ket{\psi(t)}, \qquad \ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)} . \tag{3.1}\] We use units in which the reduced Planck constant \(\hbar\) is 1, so that energies are frequencies, and times are measured in units of one over energy.1 Since \(H\) is Hermitian, \(e^{-iHt}\) is unitary. In the eigenbasis of \(H\), \(e^{-iHt} = \sum_k e^{-iE_kt}\ket{E_k}\bra{E_k}\): each eigenstate only picks up a phase, at a rate set by its energy, and a superposition changes because these phases turn at different rates. For a single qubit with \(H = -\frac{\omega}{2}Z\), for example, the relative phase of \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\) turns at the angular frequency \(\omega = E_1 - E_0 = 2\pi f_{01}\), where \(f_{01}\) is the qubit frequency of Section 1.2.2.

From a quantum computer, we get estimates of expectation values (Section 1.4.2). In this chapter, we estimate two kinds: the energy \(\langle H\rangle\) of a state that a circuit prepares (Section 3.4), and observables after a time evolution (Section 3.5 and Section 3.6). In both cases, locality is what makes the task feasible.

3.2 Hamiltonians as sums of Pauli strings

To measure a Hamiltonian, or to build a circuit from it, we write it as a sum of Pauli strings. The \(4^n\) Pauli strings on \(n\) qubits are orthogonal, \(\Tr[PQ] = 2^n\) if \(P = Q\) and \(0\) otherwise, and they form a basis of all \(2^n\times 2^n\) matrices (Section 2.1.1). Every Hamiltonian can therefore be written as \[ H = \sum_P c_P\, P, \qquad c_P = \frac{\Tr[PH]}{2^n} , \tag{3.2}\] with real coefficients, since \(c_P^* = \Tr[(PH)^\dagger]/2^n = \Tr[HP]/2^n = c_P\).

In general, all \(4^n\) coefficients can be nonzero, as many as the matrix has entries, and a sum of \(4^n\) terms is of no use on 50 qubits. In many physical systems, however, each particle interacts mainly with its neighbours, so that the Hamiltonian is a sum of terms that each involve only a few particles.

Definition 3.1 (Local Hamiltonian) A Hamiltonian on \(n\) qubits is \(k\)-local if it is a sum of terms that each act on at most \(k\) qubits, with \(k\) fixed as \(n\) grows. As a sum of Pauli strings, all its strings have weight at most \(k\).

Counting the strings of weight \(w \le k\) (choose \(w\) qubits, and \(X\), \(Y\) or \(Z\) on each) shows that a \(k\)-local Hamiltonian has at most \(\sum_{w\le k}\binom{n}{w}3^w\) terms, a polynomial in \(n\). In the models of this chapter, the terms are also local in space: each acts on neighbouring qubits of a chain or a lattice, so that every qubit appears in only a few terms, and the number of terms is proportional to \(n\). Several results below, such as the growth of the Trotter error and the light cones, rely on this stronger locality.

3.3 Spin models

Which local Hamiltonians do we study on a quantum computer? The simplest are spin models. Many particles, such as electrons, carry a spin, which makes each of them a tiny magnet. Measured along any axis, a spin gives one of two values, up or down, so it is a qubit, and the Pauli matrices \(X\), \(Y\) and \(Z\) are, up to a factor, its components along the three axes. In a magnetic material, the spins of neighbouring atoms interact. A spin model describes this with one qubit on each site of a chain or lattice, and terms that couple neighbouring qubits. Spin models are standard test problems for quantum computers: each spin is one qubit, all terms are local, and yet many of their properties, in particular their dynamics, are hard to compute classically on large lattices. The Hamiltonians of molecules can be written as sums of Pauli strings too, after an encoding of the electrons into qubits, but with many more terms; we stay with spin models.

Definition 3.2 (Transverse-field Ising model) The transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) on a chain or lattice of \(n\) qubits is \[ H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} Z_iZ_j \;-\; h\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i , \] where \(Z_i\) is \(Z\) on qubit \(i\) and the identity on the others, the first sum runs over the bonds \(\langle i,j\rangle\), the pairs of neighbouring qubits, and \(J, h > 0\) are energies. On an open chain, the bonds are the pairs \((i, i+1)\) for \(i = 1, \dots, n-1\).

Each term favours something different. A bond term \(-JZ_iZ_j\) has the energy \(-J\) when the two qubits agree, in \(\ket{00}\) or \(\ket{11}\), and \(+J\) when they differ: it favours neighbours that are aligned along \(z\). A field term \(-hX_i\) has its lowest energy in \(\ket{+}\): it favours each qubit pointing along \(x\), transverse to \(z\). No state minimises both kinds of terms at once, since \(Z\) and \(X\) do not commute. For \(h = 0\), the ground states are \(\ket{0\cdots 0}\) and \(\ket{1\cdots 1}\), and for \(J = 0\), it is \(\ket{+\cdots +}\). In between, the ground state is entangled, and on a long chain its character changes abruptly at \(h = J\), a quantum phase transition. Exercise 3.2 works out the smallest case.

On a chain of \(n\) qubits, the TFIM is a sum of \(2n-1\) Pauli strings: \(n-1\) bonds and \(n\) fields. On an \(L\times L\) square lattice (Figure 3.1), it has \(2L(L-1)\) bonds and \(L^2\) fields: 280 terms for \(L = 10\), against up to \(4^{100}\) for a general Hamiltonian on 100 qubits.

The second model of this chapter is the Heisenberg model, \[ H = J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\big(X_iX_j + Y_iY_j + Z_iZ_j\big) , \tag{3.3}\] which describes the magnetism of many materials. Each bond treats the three axes alike, so its energy depends only on how the two spins are oriented relative to each other. In the rest of the chapter, the TFIM is the running example, and the Heisenberg model appears where it makes a point more clearly.

One property of the Heisenberg model is used twice below. A bond term equals \(J(2\,\gate{SWAP} - I)\) (Exercise 3.1), so it can move a 1 from one qubit to its neighbour, but never create or remove one. The number of qubits in \(\ket{1}\), which we call the number of flipped qubits, therefore never changes during the evolution: it is a conserved quantity of the model. In particular, \(\ket{0\cdots 0}\) is an eigenstate.

3.4 Measuring observables

We now estimate the energy \(\langle H\rangle\) of a state that a circuit prepares. The examples use the Hamiltonians of Section 3.3, but everything in this section holds for any observable written as a sum of Pauli strings. Local observables, like local Hamiltonians, have few terms. An example is the magnetisation \(m = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\langle Z_i\rangle\), the average of \(\langle Z_i\rangle\) over the qubits, which measures how far the spins are aligned along \(z\).

We know from Section 1.4.2 how to estimate the expectation value of one Pauli string: rotate each qubit so that its factor becomes \(Z\), measure, and average the signs \(s = \pm 1\) of the shots. With \(N\) shots, the estimate has the standard error \(\sqrt{(1-\langle P\rangle^2)/N}\) (Equation 1.9). For a sum \(H = \sum_i c_iP_i\) of \(M\) Pauli strings, expectation values are linear, \(\langle H\rangle = \sum_i c_i\langle P_i\rangle\), so we can estimate each term and add up the estimates.

3.4.1 The shot budget

Suppose first that each term gets its own \(N_i\) shots. The estimates \(\bar s_i\) of different terms are then independent, so their variances add up, each multiplied by \(c_i^2\): \[ \mathrm{Var}\Big(\sum_i c_i\,\bar s_i\Big) = \sum_i \frac{c_i^2\sigma_i^2}{N_i} , \qquad \sigma_i^2 = 1 - \langle P_i\rangle^2 , \tag{3.4}\] where \(\sigma_i^2\) is the variance of a single shot of term \(i\). Before the experiment, we do not know the state, so we plan for the worst case, \(\sigma_i^2 \le 1\).

How should a total of \(N\) shots be split among the terms? We minimise the variance under the constraint \(\sum_i N_i = N\), treating the \(N_i\) as continuous. With a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\), we can instead minimise \(\sum_i c_i^2\sigma_i^2/N_i + \lambda\sum_i N_i\) with each \(N_i\) free, and choose \(\lambda\) at the end so that the constraint holds. Setting the derivative with respect to \(N_i\) to zero gives \(c_i^2\sigma_i^2/N_i^2 = \lambda\), so \(N_i\) is proportional to \(|c_i|\sigma_i\). With the constraint, \[ N_i = N\,\frac{|c_i|\sigma_i}{\sum_j |c_j|\sigma_j}, \qquad \mathrm{Var} = \frac{1}{N}\Big(\sum_i |c_i|\sigma_i\Big)^2 . \] A term gets shots in proportion to \(|c_i|\sigma_i\), the spread of its contribution to the energy. Since the \(\sigma_i\) are not known in advance, we can split by \(|c_i|\) alone (the worst case), or estimate the \(\sigma_i\) from a first batch of shots. To reach a standard error \(\varepsilon\), we need \[ N = \frac{\big(\sum_i |c_i|\sigma_i\big)^2}{\varepsilon^2} \;\le\; \frac{\big(\sum_i |c_i|\big)^2}{\varepsilon^2} \tag{3.5}\] shots in total. In the worst case, the cost depends only on the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients.

For the TFIM on a chain of \(n = 10\) qubits with \(J = h = 1\), \(\sum_i |c_i| = 19\), and a standard error of 0.1 takes at most \(19^2/0.1^2 = 36{,}100\) shots, spread over 19 different circuits. Switching between circuits costs time (Section 1.6), so the number of circuits matters too.

3.4.2 Measurement settings

The shots of one circuit can serve several terms at once. In Exercise 1.10, \(\langle X\otimes I\rangle\) and \(\langle X\otimes X\rangle\) could be estimated from the same shots: after a Hadamard on each qubit, every shot gives both signs. This works whenever two strings need the same rotation on every qubit on which both act.

Definition 3.3 (Qubit-wise commuting strings and measurement settings) Two Pauli strings are qubit-wise commuting if, on every qubit, their factors are equal or at least one of them is \(I\). A measurement setting assigns a basis, \(X\), \(Y\) or \(Z\), to each qubit. It measures every Pauli string whose factors agree with it wherever they are not \(I\), and these strings are qubit-wise commuting.

The TFIM needs only two settings, on any chain or lattice: in the \(Z\) setting, each shot gives the signs of all bond terms, and in the \(X\) setting, with a Hadamard on every qubit, the signs of all field terms (Figure 3.2). The Heisenberg model needs three, \(X\), \(Y\) and \(Z\) on all qubits, also on any lattice. Its terms \(X_1X_2\) and \(Y_1Y_2\) commute as matrices, but not qubit-wise, so no setting measures both; with an entangling circuit before the measurement, they could be measured together (Section 3.9).

Sharing shots changes the variance. A shot of the setting for a group \(g\) of terms gives one value of the whole group, \(s_g = \sum_{i\in g} c_i s_i\), and the mean of these values estimates \(\sum_{i\in g} c_i\langle P_i\rangle\). The terms now come from the same shots, so the single-shot variance of the group includes their covariances, which measure how two signs vary together (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019). The product \(s_is_j\) of two signs from the same shot is the sign of the Pauli string \(P_iP_j\), so that: \[ \sigma_g^2 = \mathrm{Var}(s_g) = \sum_{i,j\in g} c_ic_j\,\mathrm{Cov}(s_i,s_j) , \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(s_i, s_j) = \langle P_iP_j\rangle - \langle P_i\rangle\langle P_j\rangle . \tag{3.6}\] Different settings use different shots, so Equation 3.5 holds with groups in place of terms: \(N = (\sum_g\sigma_g)^2/\varepsilon^2\) (Exercise 3.4). In the worst case, nothing is gained: \(|s_g| \le \sum_{i\in g}|c_i|\), so \(\sigma_g \le \sum_{i\in g}|c_i|\), and the bound of Equation 3.5 stays the same. In practice, most pairs of terms in a group act on distant qubits, and in many states distant qubits are nearly uncorrelated. If all terms of a group are uncorrelated, \(\sigma_g^2 = \sum_{i\in g} c_i^2\sigma_i^2 \le \sum_{i\in g} c_i^2\): a group of \(m\) terms with equal coefficients costs up to \(m\) times fewer shots than in the worst case. For the TFIM chain with \(n = 10\), this gives \((\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{10})^2/0.1^2 \approx 3{,}800\) shots instead of 36,100, in 2 circuits instead of 19. For the state that the chain reaches from \(\ket{0\cdots0}\) after a time \(t = 1\), the exact group variances give about 3,100 shots. With data, the sample variance of the values \(s_g\) of each setting gives the standard error; Tutorial 3 does this in code.

A setting can give more than the terms it was chosen for. Each shot of the \(Z\) setting returns all \(n\) bits, and so, for the Heisenberg model, the number of flipped qubits, which the model conserves (Section 3.3). If the state was prepared with a known number of flipped qubits, every shot with a different number has suffered an error. Discarding these shots removes part of the noise, at the price of the discarded shots: it is one of the simplest ideas of error mitigation.

3.5 Trotter circuits

We now turn to dynamics: we prepare a simple state, such as \(\ket{0\cdots0}\), let it evolve under \(H\) for a time \(t\), and measure. In this section, we build circuits for \(e^{-iHt}\).

3.5.1 Simulating dynamics classically

For a few qubits, \(e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}\) can be computed exactly on a classical computer, and Tutorial 3 does so. The cost grows exponentially: the state has \(2^n\) complex amplitudes of 16 bytes each, so 30 qubits take about 17 GB of memory, and 50 qubits about 18 PB (\(1.8\times10^{16}\) bytes), close to the entire memory of the largest supercomputers.

Approximate methods avoid storing all amplitudes by using the structure of the state. For dynamics, the most widely used are tensor networks. They write the state as a network of small arrays, one per qubit, connected along the lattice, and the size of the arrays is set by the entanglement between the parts of the system. Product states, and states with little entanglement, are cheap, and so is the dynamics of a chain for short times. During an evolution, however, the entanglement typically grows in proportion to the time, and the size of the arrays grows exponentially with it, so that long times and two-dimensional lattices stay out of reach. Other methods use other structure, such as the light cones of Section 3.6. Where they all fail, a quantum computer can help, if its noise allows (Section 3.7): it holds the state in its qubits and applies \(e^{-iHt}\) as a circuit. Simulating quantum systems is the application that motivated the early proposals of quantum computers, such as Feynman (1982).

3.5.2 The Trotter formula

If all terms of \(H\) commuted, \(e^{-iHt}\) would split exactly into a product of exponentials of single terms, each a rotation about a Pauli string (Equation 1.1). For the TFIM, we split \(H = A + B\) into the bond terms \(A = -J\sum_i Z_iZ_{i+1}\) and the field terms \(B = -h\sum_i X_i\). The terms of \(A\) commute with each other, and so do those of \(B\), so \(e^{-iA\delta}\) and \(e^{-iB\delta}\) are products of rotations that a circuit can apply. But \(A\) and \(B\) do not commute, so applying first \(e^{-iA\delta}\) and then \(e^{-iB\delta}\), which is the product \(e^{-iB\delta}e^{-iA\delta}\) (the rightmost factor acts first), differs from \(e^{-i(A+B)\delta}\). For a short time \(\delta\), the difference is small. Expanding both sides to second order in \(\delta\), \[ \begin{aligned} e^{-iB\delta}e^{-iA\delta} &= I - i(A+B)\delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\big(A^2 + 2BA + B^2\big)\delta^2 + O(\delta^3) , \\ e^{-i(A+B)\delta} &= I - i(A+B)\delta - \tfrac{1}{2}\big(A^2 + AB + BA + B^2\big)\delta^2 + O(\delta^3) , \end{aligned} \] so that \[ e^{-iB\delta}e^{-iA\delta} = e^{-i(A+B)\delta} + \frac{\delta^2}{2}\,[A,B] + O(\delta^3) , \tag{3.7}\] with the commutator \([A,B] = AB - BA\). The error of one short step is of order \(\delta^2\), and it vanishes if \(A\) and \(B\) commute. To evolve for a time \(t\), we divide it into \(r\) steps of length \(\delta = t/r\): \(r\) errors of order \((t/r)^2\) add up to an error of order \(t^2/r\), which falls as the number of steps grows.

To make this precise, we need a size for matrices. The operator norm \(\|M\| = \max_{\|\psi\| = 1}\|M\ket{\psi}\|\) is the largest factor by which \(M\) can stretch the length of a vector; for a Hermitian matrix, it is the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues.

Definition 3.4 (First-order Trotter formula) For \(H = A + B\) and a time \(t\) divided into \(r\) Trotter steps of length \(\delta = t/r\), \[ e^{-iHt} \approx \big(e^{-iB\delta}e^{-iA\delta}\big)^r , \] with an error, the Trotter error, of at most \[ \Big\| e^{-iHt} - \big(e^{-iB\delta}e^{-iA\delta}\big)^r \Big\| \le \frac{t^2}{2r}\,\big\|[A,B]\big\| . \tag{3.8}\] The bound is the same for the other order, \(e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta}\).

Equation 3.8 says that, for every initial state, the state of the circuit differs from the exact one by a vector of length at most \((t^2/2r)\|[A,B]\|\). It turns the estimate of Equation 3.7 into a rigorous bound for \(r\) steps, using that the errors of the steps add up at most linearly, since unitaries do not change the length of vectors. A proof is in Childs et al. (2021, sec. V A); de Wolf (2026, sec. 9.2) derives the simpler bound \(O(\|A\|\|B\|\,t^2/r)\). With more than two groups of commuting terms, as on a square lattice, a step applies one exponential per group, with a similar bound.

For the TFIM, only terms that overlap fail to commute: \(Z_iZ_{i+1}\) anticommutes with \(X_i\) and \(X_{i+1}\), and commutes with every other \(X_j\). As a result, \(\|[A,B]\| \le 4Jh(n-1)\) (Exercise 3.6): the bound grows in proportion to the number of qubits.

3.5.3 Circuits for one step

A Trotter step of the TFIM is a product of the Pauli rotations of Equation 1.1: \(e^{iJ\delta Z_iZ_{i+1}} = R_{ZZ}(-2J\delta)\) on every bond, and \(e^{ih\delta X_i} = R_X(-2h\delta)\) on every qubit. Two bond gates that share a qubit cannot act at the same time, so the bonds are applied in two layers, first \((1,2)\), \((3,4)\), …, then \((2,3)\), \((4,5)\), …, and the field rotations in a third (Figure 3.3). Two-qubit gates on alternating pairs of neighbours form a brick-wall circuit, as in Figure 1.12.

On a device with CZ gates, each \(R_{ZZ}\) costs two CZs (Figure 1.6), so a step on a chain of \(n\) qubits costs \(2(n-1)\) CZs; on some trapped-ion devices, \(R_{ZZ}(\theta)\) is a single native gate (Table 1.3). A Trotter circuit repeats the same step \(r\) times, so a coherent error of a gate, such as an over-rotation, repeats \(r\) times too and adds up: this is the worst case of Section 2.4.

The lattice of the model must also fit the coupling graph of the device (Section 1.3.6). A chain fits on any device whose coupling graph contains a path of \(n\) qubits, and every bond is then a coupled pair. A square lattice fits directly on a device with a square coupling graph, such as IQM’s 20-qubit Garnet, with four layers of bond gates per step instead of two. On a line of qubits, we can place the lattice along a path through it (Figure 3.1 c). The bonds along the path then join neighbours, but the others join qubits up to \(2L-1\) positions apart, and each of them needs SWAPs (Figure 1.11), which multiply the cost of a step (Exercise 3.7). Trapped ions, coupled all to all, need no SWAPs.

3.5.4 Large steps: Floquet circuits

What does the circuit do when \(\delta\) is not small? Each step is still a unitary, and every unitary can be written as \(e^{-iH_F\delta}\) for some Hermitian \(H_F\), by taking the logarithms of its eigenvalues. The circuit therefore follows the exact evolution under the Hamiltonian \(H_F\), seen at the times \(t = \delta, 2\delta, \dots\) Comparing with Equation 3.7 gives (Exercise 3.8) \[ H_F = A + B + \frac{i\delta}{2}[A,B] + O(\delta^2) , \tag{3.9}\] which is close to \(H\) only for small \(\delta\). At large \(\delta\), the circuit therefore simulates a different Hamiltonian: it costs the same per step, but its dynamics can be completely different from that of \(H\). A circuit that repeats one layer of gates is called a Floquet circuit, after the mathematician Gaston Floquet, whose theory describes systems that are driven periodically in time, such as atoms under a pulsed laser. The circuit applies \(A\) and \(B\) in turn, over and over, and so acts like such a periodic drive.

Figure 3.4 shows both regimes for a TFIM chain of 6 qubits that starts in \(\ket{0\cdots0}\). Panel (a) shows the magnetisation. With steps of 0.25, it follows the exact curve. With steps of 1, it follows a different one, for example \(-0.42\) instead of \(0.20\) at \(t = 1\). Panel (b) shows the fidelity of the circuit’s state with the exact state (Definition 2.9). At steps of 0.5, the magnetisation stays within 0.1 of the exact curve, while the fidelity drops to values between 0.37 and 0.71 after the first step. The fidelity compares the whole state, and small errors in many parts of it multiply, so local observables such as the magnetisation are often much more accurate than the state as a whole.

3.6 Observables after time evolution

Once the circuit has prepared \(e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}\), we measure it. Three quantities are common, and the shots of the \(Z\) setting give all of them:

  • the magnetisation \(m\) of Section 3.4;
  • correlation functions \(\langle Z_iZ_j\rangle\), which show how much the qubits \(i\) and \(j\) agree, and how this changes with their distance;
  • the return probability \(|\bra{\psi(0)}e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}|^2\), which for \(\ket{\psi(0)} = \ket{0\cdots0}\) is the fraction of shots that give all zeros; it decreases quickly with \(n\), so large systems need many shots.

How fast does a change spread through a system? Figure 3.5 shows a Heisenberg chain of 21 qubits, with \(J = 1\), that starts in \(\ket{0\cdots0}\) with the middle qubit flipped, evolved with Trotter steps of 0.2. Each bond gate conserves the number of flipped qubits, as the Hamiltonian does (Section 3.3), so there is always exactly one flipped qubit, and \((1-\langle Z_i\rangle)/2\) is the probability of finding it on qubit \(i\). Since \(\ket{0\cdots0}\) is an eigenstate, the qubits that the flip has not reached show no change at all, so the spreading can be read off directly. Each bond gate partly swaps its two qubits (Exercise 3.1), and the flip spreads out from the middle.

The figure shows two cones, with different causes. The dashed lines are the light cone of the circuit. Each gate acts on two neighbouring qubits, and each step has two layers of bond gates, so after \(k\) steps the flip can have reached at most \(2k\) qubits on each side. Outside the dashed lines, the probability is exactly zero, up to rounding errors of order \(10^{-15}\) in the simulation. This is the cone along which errors spread in Figure 2.4, and it holds for every circuit with this layout, whatever its angles. Read backwards, it also means that a local observable after \(k\) steps depends only on the qubits inside its cone, so that short circuits can be checked by simulating only those qubits (Exercise 3.10).

The coloured region is narrower: it is the cone of the Hamiltonian itself. In a local Hamiltonian, information spreads at a finite speed, and far away from the flip nothing changes for a while, up to tails that decrease exponentially with the distance. In the Heisenberg chain of the figure, the flip travels about \(4J\) qubits per unit time, much slower than the \(2/\delta = 10\) qubits per unit time of the circuit cone. When it reaches the ends of the chain, around \(t = 2\), it is reflected, and soon it is spread over the whole chain. A circuit can only follow the physics while its cone is wider than that of the Hamiltonian, which is one more reason for small steps. Tutorial 3 asks you to rebuild this figure and Figure 3.4, with 15 qubits; there, as in Qiskit, the qubits are numbered from 0.

3.7 Trotter error against gate noise

More Trotter steps reduce the Trotter error, but each step adds gates, and with them noise. How many steps give the state closest to the exact one? We measure the distance with the fidelity of Definition 2.9, and estimate both errors with simple models.

  • Trotter error. By Equation 3.8, the state of the circuit differs from the exact one by a vector of length at most \(at^2/r\), with \(a = \|[A,B]\|/2\). For two pure states, the infidelity \(1 - F\) is at most the square of this length, so the Trotter error contributes at most \((at^2/r)^2\).
  • Gate noise. Let \(\varepsilon_{\text{step}}\) be the sum of the Pauli errors of the gates of one step. By Equation 2.12, \(r\) steps run without an error with probability about \(1 - r\varepsilon_{\text{step}}\), which approximates the fidelity with the noiseless state.

For small errors, the two infidelities add up, \[ 1 - F \approx \Big(\frac{at^2}{r}\Big)^2 + r\,\varepsilon_{\text{step}} . \tag{3.10}\] The first term falls with \(r\), and the second grows. Setting the derivative with respect to \(r\) to zero gives the best number of steps, \[ r^* = \Big(\frac{2a^2t^4}{\varepsilon_{\text{step}}}\Big)^{1/3} . \tag{3.11}\]

As an example, take a TFIM chain of 4 qubits, \(t = 1\) and \(J = h = 1\). The bound gives \(a = 2Jh(n-1) = 6\). For the noise, we take the depolarising noise model of Tutorial 2 with the values typical of current devices quoted there, \(p = 0.1\%\) after each single-qubit gate and \(0.5\%\) after each CZ, which are Pauli errors of \(3p/4\) and \(15p/16\) (Definition 2.4 and Definition 2.8). As in Tutorial 2, the circuit is compiled into PRX and CZ gates without simplification, and every single-qubit gate, \(Z\) rotations included, is a noisy pulse. One step then has 37 single-qubit gates and 6 CZs, so \(\varepsilon_{\text{step}} \approx 0.056\), and Equation 3.11 gives \(r^* \approx 11\). A simulation of the noisy circuit (Figure 3.6) finds the best fidelity, 0.82, at 3 steps instead. The bound is pessimistic for three reasons: the exact \(\|[A,B]\|\) is smaller than our estimate (8.9 instead of 12 here), the bound holds for the worst initial state, and it assumes that the errors of all steps add up. The value of \(a\) that fits the simulation without noise, \(a \approx 0.70\), gives \(r^* \approx 2.6\), close to the simulated optimum. The model therefore gives the shape of the trade-off and how it scales, while the best number of steps in practice is found by simulating small instances, as Tutorial 3 does. For local observables, which are more accurate than the state (Figure 3.4), fewer steps can be enough.
