In the previous chapters, noise was what made our estimates wrong: it shrinks expectation values (Section 2.7), it limits the number of Trotter steps (Section 3.7), and in Tutorial 4 it left the energy of the variational state far above what the ansatz could reach. In this chapter, we discuss a family of techniques developed to reduce the effect of noise in NISQ computation: error mitigation.

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to tell error suppression, mitigation and correction apart; explain how zero-noise extrapolation and symmetry verification work, and how they trade bias for standard error; and judge whether a mitigated result is plausible.

5.1 Suppression, mitigation and correction

Dealing with noise is central to quantum computing. One natural way to reduce noise is to build better hardware, with fewer errors per gate; but much can also be done on the software side, i.e. in the way we design algorithms and run them on a given device. We group the software methods to reduce error in three categories:

Definition 5.1 (Error suppression, mitigation and correction)  

  • Quantum Error suppression changes how the device runs a circuit (e.g. in gate implementation or compilation), so that each shot suffers fewer errors.
  • Quantum Error mitigation leaves the errors of each shot as they are. It runs many shots, often of several modified circuits, and combines their outcomes in classical post-processing into an estimate of the noiseless expectation value.
  • Quantum error correction encodes each logical qubit in many physical qubits, and detects and corrects errors during the computation (Section 1.1).

Suppression includes better calibrated pulses, compiling into fewer two-qubit gates, choosing the best qubits of a device, and dynamical decoupling (Section 5.2); it costs no extra shots, but it cannot remove all errors.

Mitigation includes the readout correction and the rescaling of Section 2.7.2, and the two methods explained in this chapter, as well as many more. An extensive review can be found in Cai et al. (2023). It needs no extra qubits, but it improves only estimates of expectation values, not the state on the device, and its cost in shots grows exponentially with the amount of noise (Section 5.3).

Correction can make a computation as reliable as needed, but it needs many more qubits, with error rates below the threshold (Section 1.1); in order to avoid all errors, error correction must be combined with fault-tolerant gate implementation, which is typically very expensive.

Error suppression techniques can be applied to any quantum circuit, so they combine naturally with both mitigation and correction. Combining error correction with mitigation is an active area of research, which may find applications on the first fault-tolerant devices (Dutkiewicz et al. 2025; Jeon and Cai 2026).

5.2 Error suppression

Error suppression changes how a device runs a circuit, but not what the circuit computes. Some of it happens in every run without our help: providers calibrate the pulses of the gates regularly (Section 1.2.3), and compilers turn a circuit into as few native gates as they can, above all as few two-qubit gates, which cause most errors (Section 1.3.4). In this section, we look at two techniques that we control ourselves: choosing the qubits on which a circuit runs, and protecting the qubits that wait with dynamical decoupling.

5.2.1 Choosing the qubits

No two qubits of a chip are identical, so the error rates of their gates and readouts differ, and they drift between calibrations (Section 1.2.3). Figure 5.1 (a) shows this on IQM’s 20-qubit device Garnet, whose coupling graph is a square lattice (Figure 1.10): in one set of its calibration data, the error probability of a CZ ranges from 0.7% to 1.8%.

One number measures how much noise a circuit suffers on a device: the sum of the error probabilities of all the operations it uses, gates, waits and readouts, \[ \lambda = \sum_i \varepsilon_i . \tag{5.1}\] It is the average number of errors per shot, called the circuit fault rate (Cai et al. 2023), and by Equation 2.12 a shot runs without errors with probability \(e^{-\lambda}\). Since the error rates differ, \(\lambda\) depends on which qubits of the device play the roles of the qubits of the circuit, a choice called the layout. The layout must follow the coupling graph: qubits that share two-qubit gates in the circuit should be neighbours on the device, otherwise the compiler adds SWAPs, of 3 CZs each (Section 1.3.6).

Figure 5.1 (b) shows \(\lambda\) for the circuit of Tutorial 4 on every layout of its 4-qubit chain: from 0.30 on the best layout to 0.44 on the worst. Placing the chain on QB1, QB2, QB3 and QB4, in the order of their numbers, does not follow the coupling graph, since QB2 and QB3 are not coupled: the compiler adds two SWAPs, and \(\lambda = 0.47\), more than on any layout that follows the graph.

The layout also sets the price of mitigation. Correcting the shrinking factor \(f \approx e^{-\lambda}\) by rescaling costs \(e^{2\lambda}\) times as many shots (Section 2.7.2), 1.8 on the best layout and 2.4 on the worst, and the costs of the other methods of this chapter also grow exponentially with \(\lambda\) (Section 5.3). For deeper circuits, the gap between good and bad qubits grows exponentially too (Exercise 5.1). Suppression therefore comes first, and mitigation is applied on top of it.

Compilers can choose the layout from the error rates of the device. In Qiskit, the pass VF2Layout looks for layouts that need no SWAPs and, if the backend reports error rates, picks the one with the fewest errors. By default, the IQM backend reports none, so Qiskit’s layout follows the coupling graph but ignores the calibration: for the circuit of Tutorial 4, it has \(\lambda = 0.32\) (Figure 5.1 (b)). To use the best qubits, choose them from the calibration data and fix the layout when compiling, for example with transpile(circuit, backend, initial_layout=[12, 13, 17, 18]) for QB13, QB14, QB18 and QB19 (Qiskit numbers the qubits from 0).

5.2.2 Dynamical decoupling

The circuit fault rate also counts the waits: in most circuits, some qubits wait while gates act on others, and during the wait they relax and dephase (Section 2.2.5). Dynamical decoupling fills these waits with the echo of Section 2.2.6 (Figure 5.2). An \(X\) pulse halfway through the wait reverses the sign of the phase picked up so far, so the second half of the wait undoes the first, and a second \(X\) at the end undoes the flip. Long waits are filled with several such pairs, or with other sequences of pulses whose product is the identity (Viola et al. 1999).

Dynamical decoupling only removes noise that changes slowly compared with the spacing of the pulses, and every pulse adds a small error of its own, so more pulses are not always better. Compilers can insert the pulses once the durations of all gates are known, and many sequences have been compared on superconducting devices (Ezzell et al. 2023). On IQM devices, dynamical decoupling is an option of the run (dd_mode), off by default.

5.3 The price of mitigation

All error mitigation methods combine estimates from noisy circuits into a new estimate, with a smaller bias and a larger variance. We met the simplest case in Section 2.7.2. If the noise shrinks an expectation value by a known factor \(f\), as global depolarising noise does (Equation 2.9), the rescaled estimate \(\bar s/f\) has no bias, and its standard error is that of \(\bar s\) multiplied by \(1/f\). By Equation 2.13, rescaling changes the mean squared error from \[ (1-f)^2\langle P\rangle^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{N} \qquad\text{to}\qquad \frac{\sigma^2}{f^2N} , \tag{5.2}\] where \(\sigma^2 = 1 - f^2\langle P\rangle^2\) is the variance of a single shot of the noisy circuit. Rescaling replaces a bias, which no number of shots removes, by a larger variance, which shots do remove: it pays off once there are enough shots for the bias to matter (Exercise 5.2).

To reach the same standard error as the unmitigated estimate, rescaling needs \(1/f^2\) times as many shots. This factor is the sampling overhead of a method: the number of shots it needs for a given standard error, divided by the number that the unmitigated estimate needs. By Equation 2.12, \(f \approx e^{-\lambda}\), with the circuit fault rate \(\lambda\) of Equation 5.1, so the overhead of rescaling is \(e^{2\lambda}\): about 7 for \(\lambda = 1\), 400 for \(\lambda = 3\) and 22,000 for \(\lambda = 5\). Other methods have other overheads, but for general circuits all of them grow exponentially with \(\lambda\), and it has been proved that, in the worst case, no method of a broad class, which includes those of this chapter, can avoid this (Quek et al. 2024). Mitigation therefore works for circuits whose fault rate is at most of order 1 (Cai et al. 2023): it extends what a noisy device can do, but it does not remove the limit set by noise.

Readout errors (Section 2.5) are the easiest case, because they act only at the end of the circuit and their rates can be measured: prepare \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\) and read them out many times, as in Tutorial 2. Inverting Equation 2.10 gives the corrected value of a single qubit, \[ \langle Z\rangle = \frac{\langle Z\rangle_{\text{read}} - (e_1 - e_0)}{1 - e_0 - e_1} , \] a rescaling with an offset. For independent readouts on many qubits, the inverse of each qubit’s confusion matrix is applied to the measured probabilities of the bit strings, one qubit at a time. Each qubit’s correction multiplies the standard error by about \(1/(1 - e_0 - e_1)\). This readout correction is the first step of rescaling and of ZNE; symmetry verification instead discards most readout errors with the other errors (Exercise 5.4).

Example. In Tutorial 4, the optimised HVA circuit for a TFIM chain of 4 qubits (\(J = 1\), \(h = 0.5\), 3 layers) reaches an energy only 0.003 above \(E_0 = -3.427\) without noise. Compiled into 58 single-qubit gates and 18 CZs, under depolarising noise of 0.1% after each single-qubit gate and 0.5% after each CZ, its energy is 0.277 above \(E_0\), almost all of it bias. The error budget, with the Pauli errors \(3p/4\) and \(15p/16\) of these channels (Definition 2.4 and Definition 2.8), gives \(\lambda \approx 0.13\) and \(f \approx 0.88\). Dividing the noisy energy by this \(f\) gives an energy 0.15 below \(E_0\): rescaling over-corrects, and the result breaks the variational bound (Equation 4.1). The noise is not global depolarising: not every error changes every term of \(H\), each term shrinks by its own factor, and the energy as a whole shrinks by 0.92, not 0.88. Only a simulation of the noisy circuit could have predicted this.

As in Section 2.7.2, the difficulty of rescaling lies in knowing \(f\), and the two methods of this chapter avoid it in different ways. Zero-noise extrapolation measures how the estimate changes when the noise grows, instead of predicting it. Symmetry verification recognises the shots that suffered an error, instead of correcting all shots on average.

5.4 Zero-noise extrapolation

Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) does the opposite of suppression: it increases the noise of the circuit, in a controlled way. It estimates an expectation value at several noise levels, all at least as large as the device’s own, fits a curve through these estimates, and evaluates it at zero noise (Temme et al. 2017; Li and Benjamin 2017). It needs no model of the noise, only a way to scale it.

5.4.1 Scaling the noise by folding

Write \(E(c)\) for the expectation value of the observable, for example the energy, measured when every error of the circuit is made \(c\) times more likely, so that \(E(1)\) is the noisy value that we can measure and \(E(0)\) the noiseless one that we want; \(c\) is called the noise scale factor. On a digital device, the simplest way to scale the noise is gate folding: replace a gate \(G\) by \(G G^\dagger G\) (Giurgica-Tiron et al. 2020). In the ideal circuit, \(G^\dagger G = I\), so nothing changes; on the device, the gate runs three times, with three times the errors, if \(G^\dagger\) is as noisy as \(G\). Folding the whole circuit \(U\) into \(U(U^\dagger U)^k\) scales the noise by \(c = 2k+1\), that is, by 1, 3, 5, …. Folding only \(m\) of its \(G\) gates, chosen at random, gives the factors in between, \(c = 1 + 2m/G\) if all gates are equally noisy; in practice, one often folds only the two-qubit gates, which dominate the error budget, and counts \(G\) among them.

Three points matter when implementing folding.

  1. Fold the circuit that prepares the state, after compiling it into native gates, and only then add the basis changes of the measurement settings and the measurements, as in Tutorial 4. Make sure that nothing simplifies the folded circuit afterwards: a compiler that sees \(U^\dagger U\) removes it. In Qiskit, the folded circuit is native.compose(native.inverse()).compose(native), built before adding measurements and run without compiling again. With r and cz, the inverse of each gate is a gate of the same kind, so the noise model acts on it too; if the inverse contains gates that are not native, compile again with barriers between the three parts, which keep the compiler from cancelling them.
  2. Folding scales only the errors of the gates. Readout errors are the same at every \(c\), so they should be corrected first (Section 5.3), or they remain in the extrapolated value.
  3. Folding assumes that errors add up, like the incoherent errors of Section 2.4. Coherent errors can instead cancel: if every pulse over-rotates by a fixed fraction of its angle, \(G^\dagger = R_X(-\theta)\) over-rotates in the opposite direction to \(G = R_X(\theta)\), and \(GG^\dagger G\) has the error of a single \(G\). Twirling (Section 2.4) turns coherent errors into Pauli channels, which do add up (Cai et al. 2023).

5.4.2 Extrapolating to zero noise

With estimates \(\hat E_j\) at the scale factors \(c_1 < c_2 < \dots < c_m\), we choose a model for \(E(c)\), fit it, and evaluate it at \(c = 0\). Three models are common.

  1. Linear, \(E(c) = a + bc\). From two points, \[ \hat E_{\text{lin}} = \frac{c_2\hat E_1 - c_1\hat E_2}{c_2 - c_1} , \] which for \(c = 1, 3\) is \((3\hat E_1 - \hat E_3)/2\).
  2. Polynomial: the polynomial of degree \(m-1\) through all \(m\) points, evaluated at \(c = 0\). This is called Richardson extrapolation, after the mathematician and meteorologist Lewis Fry Richardson. Like the linear case (\(m = 2\)), it is a linear combination of the estimates, \[ \hat E_{\text{R}} = \sum_{j=1}^m \gamma_j\hat E_j , \qquad \gamma_j = \prod_{k\ne j}\frac{c_k}{c_k - c_j} , \tag{5.3}\] for example \(\gamma = (\tfrac{15}{8}, -\tfrac{5}{4}, \tfrac{3}{8})\) for \(c = 1, 3, 5\).
  3. Exponential, \(E(c) = Ae^{-bc}\), the form that global depolarising noise gives, \(E(c) = f^cE(0)\) by Equation 2.9. From two estimates with the same sign, \[ \hat E_{\text{exp}} = \hat E_1\Big(\frac{\hat E_1}{\hat E_2}\Big)^{c_1/(c_2 - c_1)} , \] which for \(c = 1, 3\) is \(\hat E_1(\hat E_1/\hat E_3)^{1/2}\) (Endo et al. 2018). This model assumes that noise pulls the value towards zero, and it fails when an estimate is within its standard error of zero. Depolarising noise pulls an observable \(O\) towards its value in the maximally mixed state, \(\Tr[O]/2^n\), which must be subtracted first if it is not zero; for the TFIM, \(\Tr[H] = 0\). Since this model is not linear, it matters what we extrapolate: here, always the total energy.

With more points than parameters, a least-squares fit replaces these formulas. Which model fits best depends on the noise. Under global depolarising noise, the exponential model is exact, while the linear one leaves a bias of order \(\lambda^2\) (Exercise 5.3) and Richardson extrapolation with \(m\) points one of order \(\lambda^m\).

5.4.3 The price: amplified shot noise

The estimates at different scale factors come from different shots, so they are independent, and the variance of a linear combination of them is (Equation 3.5) \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat E_{\text{R}}) = \sum_j \gamma_j^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat E_j) . \tag{5.4}\] The coefficients sum to 1, since a constant \(E(c)\) must extrapolate to itself, but the coefficient of the smallest scale factor, \(\gamma_1 = \prod_{k\ne 1} c_k/(c_k - c_1)\), is larger than 1, so \(\sum_j\gamma_j^2 > 1\): the extrapolation amplifies the shot noise. For \(c = 1, 3\), \(\sum_j\gamma_j^2 = 9/4 + 1/4 = 2.5\), and for \(c = 1, 3, 5\) it is about 5.2. With the same number of shots at every point, the standard error therefore grows by a factor of 1.6 and 2.3. Spreading the scale factors further apart makes the \(\gamma_j\) smaller, but then the model must hold over a longer range, and the signal at large \(c\) is smaller. More points cancel more of the bias, but amplify the shot noise more. For the exponential model with \(c = 1, 3\), propagating the errors to first order gives \[ \mathrm{Var}(\hat E_{\text{exp}}) \approx \frac{9}{4}\,\frac{\hat E_1}{\hat E_3}\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat E_1) + \frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{\hat E_1}{\hat E_3}\Big)^3\mathrm{Var}(\hat E_3) , \] and under global depolarising noise \(\hat E_1/\hat E_3 \approx e^{2\lambda}\), so the overhead grows exponentially with \(\lambda\), as for rescaling. For any other fit, the standard error can be found by resampling: repeat the extrapolation many times on values drawn at random from Gaussians with the measured estimates as means and their standard errors as standard deviations, and take the standard deviation of the results.

Example. Figure 5.3 shows ZNE for the energy of the Tutorial 4 circuit, folded as a whole into circuits with 18, 54 and 90 CZs (\(c = 1, 3, 5\)). Panel (a) shows the exact noisy energies, without shots: 0.277, 0.759 and 1.164 above \(E_0\), on a curve that is nearly straight. Linear extrapolation from \(c = 1, 3\) gives 0.036 above \(E_0\), Richardson extrapolation from all three points 0.008, and exponential extrapolation from \(c = 1, 3\) gives 0.004, close to the 0.003 of the noiseless circuit: the simulation has depolarising noise after every gate, so each term of the energy decays nearly exponentially with \(c\).

Panel (b) repeats the experiment 1000 times, with 1000 shots per setting at each scale factor: 2,000 shots in total without mitigation, 4,000 for the linear and exponential extrapolations, and 6,000 for Richardson’s. Without mitigation, the estimates scatter with a standard deviation of 0.05 around the exact 0.277 above \(E_0\). With mitigation, the bias almost disappears, but the scatter grows to 0.08 (linear), 0.12 (Richardson) and 0.09 (exponential), as Equation 5.4 and the propagation of errors predict, and a third to a half of the mitigated estimates fall below \(E_0\). All three are two to three times closer to \(E_0\) than the unmitigated estimate, in root-mean-square error. With ten times more shots, the remaining bias of the linear fit would be about as large as its standard error.

In practice, ZNE takes the following steps.

  1. Compile the circuit into native gates, and build the folded circuits for the chosen scale factors, for example \(c = 1, 3, 5\), protected from further simplification.
  2. Estimate the expectation value and its standard error at each \(c\), with the same measurement settings, and correct readout errors in each estimate.
  3. Extrapolate with one or more models, and propagate the standard errors (Equation 5.4).
  4. Check the result: if the models disagree by more than their standard errors, the extrapolation is not reliable. Where possible, test the procedure on a small instance with a known answer and the same noise.

In a VQE, ZNE is usually applied only to the final angles: inside the optimisation loop, it would multiply the cost of every step, while the optimiser only needs to compare energies.

5.5 Symmetry verification

Zero-noise extrapolation uses nothing about the problem. Symmetry verification instead uses a property of the problem, a symmetry, to recognise the shots that suffered an error, and discards them.

5.5.1 Symmetries and quantum numbers

We met two conserved quantities in earlier chapters, and called them symmetries in Section 4.1.1: the number of flipped qubits of the Heisenberg model (Section 3.3), and the parity of the TFIM, the flip of all qubits \(X_1X_2\cdots X_n\) (Section 4.2). Both are observables that commute with the Hamiltonian.

Definition 5.2 (Symmetry, quantum number and sector) A symmetry of a Hamiltonian \(H\) is an observable \(S\) that commutes with it, \(SH = HS\). The eigenvalues \(s\) of \(S\) are called quantum numbers, and the eigenspace of each eigenvalue, made of the states with \(S\ket{\psi} = s\ket{\psi}\), is a symmetry sector.

The name comes from physics, which labels states by their conserved values, for example the number of electrons of a molecule or its total spin. Three properties follow from \(SH = HS\).

  1. \(S\) commutes with every power of \(H\), and so with \(e^{-iHt}\). A state in the sector \(s\) stays there: \(Se^{-iHt}\ket{\psi} = e^{-iHt}S\ket{\psi} = s\,e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi}\). The quantum number is conserved.
  2. \(H\) maps each sector into itself: if \(S\ket{\psi} = s\ket{\psi}\), then \(S(H\ket{\psi}) = H(S\ket{\psi}) = s\,H\ket{\psi}\). In a basis of eigenvectors of \(S\), sorted by sector, the matrix of \(H\) is block diagonal (Figure 5.4), and the eigenvectors of \(H\) can be found one block at a time, each with a definite quantum number.
  3. A circuit whose gates all commute with \(S\) conserves the quantum number too, even if it does not follow the exact evolution. The bond gates of the Heisenberg Trotter circuit conserve the number of flipped qubits (Section 3.6), and the \(R_{ZZ}\) and \(R_X\) gates of the HVA commute with the parity (Section 4.2).

For the Heisenberg chain, the number of flipped qubits is the observable \[ K = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{I - Z_i}{2} , \] which counts the 1s of a basis state: \(K\ket{x} = k\ket{x}\) for a bit string \(x\) with \(k\) ones. Its sector \(k\) is spanned by the \(\binom{n}{k}\) bit strings with \(k\) ones. Each bond term equals \(J(2\,\gate{SWAP} - I)\) (Exercise 3.1), so it moves 1s around but never creates or removes one. For the TFIM, the parity \(\Pi = X_1X_2\cdots X_n\) squares to \(I\), so its eigenvalues are \(\pm 1\), and its two sectors have \(2^{n-1}\) states each. Each \(X_i\) commutes with \(\Pi\), and so does each \(Z_iZ_{i+1}\), which anticommutes with two of its factors.

5.5.2 Post-selection on the quantum number

Suppose that the circuit starts in a state with the quantum number \(s_0\), and that all its gates commute with \(S\). Without noise, the final state lies in the sector \(s_0\), and a measurement of \(S\) would give \(s_0\) in every shot. An error that does not commute with \(S\) can move the state to another sector, where the following gates keep it. If the quantum number can be read from the outcomes of each shot, a shot that gives a value other than \(s_0\) reveals that an error happened. Symmetry verification discards these shots, and estimates the expectation value from the others (Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018; McArdle et al. 2019). Keeping only the shots that pass a test is called post-selection.

Symmetry verification needs three conditions:

  1. the Hamiltonian, and every gate of the circuit before compiling, conserve \(S\) (the native gates of the compiled circuit need not, one by one). A Pauli rotation \(e^{-i\theta P/2}\) conserves a Pauli string \(S\) if \(P\) and \(S\) commute, that is, if they anticommute on an even number of qubits;
  2. the initial state has a definite quantum number \(s_0\);
  3. the measurement reveals \(S\): in the measured basis, \(S\) is diagonal, so that the bits of each shot give its value. For \(K\), measured in the \(Z\) basis, it is the number of 1s; for a Pauli string such as the parity, it is the product of the signs of its qubits, in a measurement setting that agrees with it on every qubit (Definition 3.5). For the parity in the \(X\) setting, a shot passes if it has an even number of 1s.

We now quantify the gain and the price. As in Equation 2.12, a shot runs without errors with probability \(f \approx e^{-\lambda}\); this \(f\) equals the shrinking factor of expectation values only for scrambling noise (Section 2.6.2), which is why the two differed in the example of Section 5.3. Of the shots with errors, suppose that a fraction \(d\) is detected. The fraction of shots kept is then \[ p_{\text{keep}} = f + (1-f)(1-d) , \] and among them, a fraction \(f/p_{\text{keep}}\) ran without errors. If \(\langle O\rangle_u\) is the average of the observable over the shots with undetected errors, the post-selected estimate averages to \[ \langle O\rangle_{\text{ps}} = \frac{f\langle O\rangle + (1-f)(1-d)\langle O\rangle_u}{p_{\text{keep}}} , \tag{5.5}\] so its bias, \((1-f)(1-d)\big(\langle O\rangle_u - \langle O\rangle\big)/p_{\text{keep}}\), comes only from the undetected errors. The price is that only \(p_{\text{keep}}N\) of the \(N\) shots are used, so the variance grows to about \(\sigma^2/(p_{\text{keep}}N)\), by a factor \(1/p_{\text{keep}}\). If every error is detected, \(d = 1\), the estimate has no bias, and its sampling overhead is \(1/f \approx e^{\lambda}\), the square root of the overhead \(e^{2\lambda}\) of rescaling. Post-selection is cheaper because it knows which shots went wrong, while rescaling corrects all shots on average. The fraction of discarded shots, \((1-f)d\), is also a measured lower bound on the probability of an error, a useful check on the error budget.

Example. Figure 5.5 repeats the light cone of Figure 3.6 for a Heisenberg chain of 11 qubits (\(J = 1\), Trotter steps of 0.2), now with noise: the depolarising noise of Tutorial 4, 0.1% per single-qubit gate and 0.5% per CZ, and readout errors \(e_0 = 1\%\) and \(e_1 = 2\%\). Each bond gate compiles into 3 CZs (with optimization_level=2; level 1, as in Tutorial 3, gives 6), so after 10 steps the circuit has 300 CZs, and \(\lambda \approx 2.3\). We measure only in the \(Z\) basis, and estimate the probability of finding the flip on each qubit from 4000 shots at each time. Without mitigation (panel b), errors create flips everywhere: at \(t = 2\), a shot shows 2.5 flipped qubits on average instead of one, and the probabilities on the qubits are off by 0.14 on average. Each shot also gives \(K\) for free, as the number of 1s in its bit string, and we keep the shots with exactly one. After post-selection (panel c), the cone is back, and the error at \(t = 2\) falls to 0.02. What remains comes from undetected errors, such as \(Z\) errors, which change the phases of the state but not the number of flips.

Panel (d) shows the price. The fraction of shots kept falls from 0.90 at \(t = 0\), where only readout errors act, to 0.23 at \(t = 2\), so at \(t = 2\) the variance is 4.4 times larger than with all shots. Rescaling, if \(f\) were known, would cost \(e^{2\lambda} \approx 100\). The gap between the two curves of panel (d) is the fraction of shots with undetected errors.

In practice, symmetry verification takes the following steps.

  1. Find a symmetry \(S\) of the problem that every gate of the circuit conserves, and the quantum number \(s_0\) of the initial state.
  2. For each measurement setting, check whether \(S\) can be read from its outcomes. The settings in which it cannot are used with all their shots.
  3. For each shot of such a setting, compute the quantum number from its bits, and keep the shot if it equals \(s_0\).
  4. Estimate the observable from the kept shots, with the standard error for their number, \(N_{\text{kept}}\) instead of \(N\) (Equation 1.9).
  5. Report the fraction of shots kept. Other methods can be applied on top: ZNE, for example, extrapolates post-selected estimates as well as plain ones.

Post-selection needs no extra circuits, so in a VQE it can also be used inside the optimisation loop.

Exercise 5.4 (Post-selection against readout errors) The circuit of Figure 5.5 ends with one flipped qubit among \(n\). Suppose that its gates are perfect, and that the readout of each qubit reports the wrong bit with probability \(e\), independently.

  1. Show that every shot with exactly one readout error is discarded by post-selection on one flipped qubit. Which shots with two readout errors are kept, and what do they report?

  2. Show that the fraction of shots kept is \((1-e)^n + (n-1)e^2(1-e)^{n-2}\).

  3. For \(n = 11\) and \(e = 1\%\), compute the fraction of shots kept, and compare it with panel (d) of Figure 5.5 at \(t = 0\). Which fraction of the kept shots report the flip on a wrong qubit, and which fraction of all shots have at least one readout error?

5.5.3 Verifying the energy of the TFIM

For the VQE of Tutorial 4, the symmetry is the parity: the circuit starts in \(\ket{+\cdots+}\), with parity \(+1\), and every gate of the HVA commutes with \(\Pi\). But the parity can only be read in the \(X\) setting of the TFIM, where each shot gives the signs of all \(X_i\), and \(\Pi\) is their product (Figure 3.3). In the \(Z\) setting, which gives the bond terms, \(\Pi\) is not diagonal, and the shots carry no information about it. So only the field terms can be verified. The errors that the check catches are those that anticommute with \(\Pi\), such as \(Z\) or \(Y\) on one qubit. An \(X_i\) error commutes with \(\Pi\), so it goes undetected, and it flips the signs of \(Z_{i-1}Z_i\) and \(Z_iZ_{i+1}\): it changes exactly the bond terms that we cannot verify.

Example. For the circuit of Tutorial 4, the check discards 6% of the shots of the \(X\) setting, and the bias of the field terms falls from 0.073 to 0.040. The bond terms keep their bias of 0.20, so the bias of the energy only falls from 0.277 to 0.244.

Settings with an entangling circuit before the measurement can do better (Section 3.4.2). On two neighbouring qubits, \(Z_iZ_{i+1}\) and \(X_iX_{i+1}\) commute, and a CNOT from qubit \(i\) to \(i+1\) followed by a Hadamard on qubit \(i\) turns them into \(Z_{i+1}\) and \(Z_i\), respectively, so one shot gives both signs. Measuring the pairs \((1,2)\), \((3,4)\), … in this way gives their bond terms and, as the product of all \(X_iX_{i+1}\), the parity; a second setting does the same for the pairs \((2,3)\), \((4,5)\), …, with the qubits left without a pair measured in the \(X\) basis. With these two settings and the \(X\) setting, every term is verified, at the cost of a third setting and one noisy CNOT per pair; for the circuit of Tutorial 4, the bias falls to 0.17. It does not fall further because \(X\) errors still escape the check, while they flip the signs of the bond terms.

Symmetry verification is therefore not always available. It needs a symmetry that the circuit conserves, which many circuits do not have: a hardware-efficient ansatz (Section 4.3.3), for example, breaks the parity of the TFIM. And it needs a measurement that reveals the symmetry, which may hold for some settings only. Symmetries also differ in how many errors they catch. The number of flipped qubits keeps the state in one sector of \(n\) states out of \(2^n\), 11 out of 2048 for our chain, while the parity only splits the states into two halves. Two errors that each flip the parity cancel each other, while two errors that each change the number of flipped qubits rarely bring it back to one (Exercise 5.5).

Exercise 5.5 (Strong and weak symmetries) We compare two checks: the number of flipped qubits \(K\) of a Heisenberg chain with one flip, read in the \(Z\) basis, and the parity \(\Pi\) of the TFIM, read in the \(X\) basis.

  1. Just before the measurement, one qubit suffers an error \(X\), \(Y\) or \(Z\), each with probability \(1/3\). With which probability does each check detect it?

  2. Now two different qubits each suffer such an error, independently. Show that the parity check detects the pair with probability \(4/9\). With which probability does the check of \(K\) detect it, if neither error hits the flipped qubit?

  3. After many errors, the state is close to a random state of the \(n\) qubits. With which probability does it pass each check, for \(n = 11\)?

5.6 Reading a mitigated result

A mitigated value is the output of a model and of post-processing, and it should be read with both in mind. Five questions help.

  1. Does it come with a standard error that includes the amplified shot noise (Equation 5.4, or the shots kept)? Without one, it cannot be judged.
  2. Which model does it assume: which fit and scale factors, and do other fits agree? For rescaling, where does \(f\) come from?
  3. For post-selection, which fraction of the shots was kept?
  4. Does it break a bound that the true value satisfies? The noisy state is still a state, a mixed one, so its energy satisfies the variational bound (Equation 4.1). A mitigated energy is a rescaled estimate or an extrapolation, and need not satisfy it: an energy below \(E_0\) after mitigation (Exercise 4.2) is amplified shot noise, as in Figure 5.3, or an over-correction, as in the rescaling example of Section 5.3.
  5. Was the method tested on an instance with a known answer and the same noise?

5.7 Other methods

Many other methods exist; Cai et al. (2023) review them, and we may return to some later in the course.

  • Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) undoes the noise of each gate on average, by inserting random operations with positive and negative weights (Temme et al. 2017); it needs an accurate noise model, learned from the device.
  • Twirling (Section 2.4) does not remove errors, but makes them simpler to model, which PEC needs and which helps ZNE (Section 5.4.1).
  • Purification methods, such as virtual distillation (Huggins et al. 2021; Koczor 2021), use several copies of the state to suppress the contribution of errors.
  • Learning-based methods learn the relation between noisy and noiseless values on circuits that can be simulated classically (Czarnik et al. 2021).

5.8 Further reading

Cai et al. (2023) is the main review of error mitigation, with the circuit fault rate and the sampling overhead used in this chapter. Kandala et al. (2019) applied zero-noise extrapolation to VQE on a superconducting device, scaling the noise by stretching the pulses, and Kim et al. (2023) used it on circuits of 127 qubits. Software packages such as Mitiq (LaRose et al. 2022) implement several of these methods for circuits written in different SDKs.
